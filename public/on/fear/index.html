What the classics wrote about fear

Most decisions that stall are stalled by fear of a loss that has not happened yet. Several of these writers separate the event from the judgment we make about it.

Every line below appears word for word in the public-domain translation named under it; a script checked each one against the text of that edition. The link opens the letter, book or edition it came from.

Seneca

“No evil is great which is the last evil of all.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 4 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“The present alone can make no man wretched.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 5 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“That man is happiest, and is secure in his own possession of himself, who can await the morrow without apprehension.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 12 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 13 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Without it, no one can live fearlessly or in peace of mind.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 16 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“If you would not have a man flinch when the crisis comes, train him before it comes.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 18 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“There is no fixed count of our years. You do not know where death awaits you; so be ready for it everywhere.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 26 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“There is only one chain which binds us to life, and that is the love of life.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 26 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“No servitude is more disgraceful than that which is self-imposed.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 47 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source

Marcus Aurelius

“If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgment about it. And it is in thy power to wipe out this judgment now.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VIII.47 · trans. George Long · source
“The soul of man does violence to itself, first of all, when it becomes an abscess, and, as it were, a tumor on the universe, so far as it can.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, II.16 · trans. George Long · source
“The universe is transformation: life is opinion.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, IV.3 · trans. George Long · source
“He then who fears or is grieved or is angry is a runaway.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, X.25 · trans. George Long · source
“Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink? art thou angry with him whose mouth smells foul? What good will this anger do thee?”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, V.28 · trans. George Long · source
“Why then am I angry? Dost thou think that a false opinion has less power than the bile in the jaundiced or the poison in him who is bitten by a mad dog?”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.57 · trans. George Long · source

Socrates

“For the fear of death is indeed the pretence of wisdom, and not real wisdom, being a pretence of knowing the unknown; and no one knows whether death, which men in their fear apprehend to be the greatest evil, may not be the greatest good.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Apology · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Leave me then, Crito, to fulfil the will of God, and to follow whither he leads.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Crito · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Can any man be courageous who has the fear of death in him?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic III · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Reflect: our principle is that the good man will not consider death terrible to any other good man who is his comrade.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic III · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Behold! human beings living in a underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light and reaching all along the den”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic VII · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“The law would say that to be patient under suffering is best, and that we should not give way to impatience, as there is no knowing whether such things are good or evil”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic X · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source

Maria Montessori

“the fear of not achieving promotion, withholds the clerk from running away, and binds him to his monotonous work, even as the fear of not passing into the next class drives the pupil to his book.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“he who accomplishes a truly human work, he who does something really great and victorious, is never spurred to his task by those trifling attractions called by the name of "prizes," nor by the fear of those petty ills which we call "punishments."”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“As for punishments, the soul of the normal man grows perfect through expanding, and punishment as commonly understood is always a form of _repression_.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“Once we have accepted and established such principles, the abolition of prizes and external forms of punishment will follow naturally.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“An armed brigand stops the carriage with the well-known phrase, "Your money or your life."”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source

The Buddha

“It's when a gentleman owes no debt, large or small, to anyone.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 4.62:5.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Furthermore, with his legitimate wealth he protects himself against losses from such things as fire, water, kings, bandits, or unloved heirs. He keeps himself safe.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 5.41:3.1–3.3 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Mendicants, isn't poverty suffering in the world for a person who enjoys sensual pleasures?”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 6.45:1.1 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Sorrowless, stainless, secure: this is the highest freedom from debt.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 6.45:28.3–28.4 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source

Confucius

“The reason why the ancients did not readily give utterance to their words, was that they feared lest their actions should not come up to them.”Confucius, Analects, IV.22 · trans. James Legge · source
“Learn as if you could not reach your object, and were always fearing also lest you should lose it.”Confucius, Analects, VIII.17 · trans. James Legge · source
“He who does not anticipate attempts to deceive him, nor think beforehand of his not being believed, and yet apprehends these things readily (when they occur);-- is he not a man of superior worth?”Confucius, Analects, XIV.33 · trans. James Legge · source
“If a man take no thought about what is distant, he will find sorrow near at hand.”Confucius, Analects, XV.11 · trans. James Legge · source

Benjamin Franklin

“My distemper was a pleurisy, which very nearly carried me off”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. VII (Beginning Business in Philadelphia) · source
“I had conceiv'd some doubts and some fears for the event of the campaign”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. XVI (Braddock's Expedition) · source
