What the classics wrote about failure

Mistakes, starting again, and the faults we keep repeating. Failure read as information rather than as a verdict.

Every line below appears word for word in the public-domain translation named under it; a script checked each one against the text of that edition. The link opens the letter, book or edition it came from.

Maria Montessori

I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“The real punishment of normal man is the loss of the consciousness of that individual power and greatness which are the sources of his inner life.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“progress comes from the _new things that are born_, and these, not being foreseen, are not rewarded with prizes: rather, they often carry the leader to martyrdom.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. I "A Critical Consideration of the New Pedagogy in its Relation to Modern Science" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“He is an individual _annihilated_, not _disciplined_.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“not to _insist_ by repeating the lesson; and second, _not to make the child feel that he has made a mistake_, or that he is not understood”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. VI "How the Lessons Should Be Given" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“it is precisely in these errors that the educational importance of the didactic material lies, and when the child with evident security places each piece in its proper place, he has outgrown the exercise, and this piece of material becomes useless to him.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XII "Education of the Senses" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“would require again the _active_ work of the _teacher_, who busies herself furnishing knowledge, and making haste to correct every error in order that the child may _learn the use of the objects_.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XII "Education of the Senses" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“the _silence_ which follows the error leaves the field of consciousness clear, and the next lesson may successfully follow the first.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XV "Intellectual Education" · trans. Anne E. George · source

Socrates

“Wherefore, O judges, be of good cheer about death, and know of a certainty, that no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Apology · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“I only wish it were so, Crito; and that the many could do the greatest evil; for then they would also be able to do the greatest good-- and what a fine thing this would be!”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Crito · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Crito · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Or, in spite of the opinion of the many, and in spite of consequences whether better or worse, shall we insist on the truth of what was then said, that injustice is always an evil and dishonour to him who acts unjustly?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Crito · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“As to my being a torpedo, if the torpedo is torpid as well as the cause of torpidity in others, then indeed I am a torpedo, but not otherwise; for I perplex others, not because I am clear, but because I am utterly perplexed myself.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Meno · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Evils, Theodorus, can never pass away; for there must always remain something which is antagonistic to good.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Theaetetus · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Can there be any greater evil than discord and distraction and plurality where unity ought to reign? or any greater good than the bond of unity?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic V · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“The law would say that to be patient under suffering is best, and that we should not give way to impatience, as there is no knowing whether such things are good or evil”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic X · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source

“He who does not rouse himself when it is time to rise, who, though young and strong, is full of sloth, whose will and thought are weak, that lazy and idle man will never find the way to knowledge.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 280 · trans. “Of this one thing make sure against your dying day,—let your faults die before you die.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 27 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
