What the classics wrote about money

Enough, debt, and what money is for. Franklin is practical, Seneca is suspicious of it, Confucius asks how it was gained.

Every line below appears word for word in the public-domain translation named under it; a script checked each one against the text of that edition. The link opens the letter, book or edition it came from.

Seneca

“It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 2 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“He who has made a fair compact with poverty is rich.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 4 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“It is the sign of an unstable mind not to be able to endure riches.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 5 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“It matters little whether the house be built of turf, or of variously coloured imported marble; understand that a man is sheltered just as well by a thatch as by a roof of gold.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 8 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“He who begins to be your friend because it pays will also cease because it pays.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 9 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Natural desires are limited; but those which spring from false opinion can have no stopping-point.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 16 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Hunger costs but little; squeamishness costs much. Poverty is contented with fulfilling pressing needs.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 17 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Study cannot be helpful unless you take pains to live simply; and living simply is voluntary poverty.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 17 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Establish business relations with poverty.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 18 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“No man is able to borrow or buy a sound mind; in fact, as it seems to me, even though sound minds were for sale, they would not find buyers. Depraved minds, however, are bought and sold every day.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 27 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“A thatched roof once covered free men; under marble and gold dwells slavery.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 90 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source

The Buddha

“He himself does not belong to himself; how much less sons and wealth?”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 62 · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“It's when a gentleman owns legitimate wealth that he has earned by his own efforts and initiative, built up with his own hands, gathered by the sweat of the brow.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 4.62:3.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“It's when a gentleman owes no debt, large or small, to anyone.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 4.62:5.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Furthermore, with his legitimate wealth he protects himself against losses from such things as fire, water, kings, bandits, or unloved heirs. He keeps himself safe.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 5.41:3.1–3.3 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“It's when a gentleman, knowing his income and expenditure, balances his finances, being neither too extravagant nor too frugal.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 8.54:5.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Mendicants, isn't poverty suffering in the world for a person who enjoys sensual pleasures?”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 6.45:1.1 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Sorrowless, stainless, secure: this is the highest freedom from debt.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 6.45:28.3–28.4 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“an ethical person gains great wealth on account of diligence.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, DN 16:1.24.3 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“One portion is to enjoy. Two parts invest in work. And the fourth should be kept for times of trouble.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, DN 31:26.19–22 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source

Confucius

“The mind of the superior man is conversant with righteousness; the mind of the mean man is conversant with gain.”Confucius, Analects, IV.16 · trans. James Legge · source
“If the search for riches is sure to be successful, though I should become a groom with whip in hand to get them, I will do so.”Confucius, Analects, VII.11 · trans. James Legge · source
“With coarse rice to eat, with water to drink, and my bended arm for a pillow;-- I have still joy in the midst of these things.”Confucius, Analects, VII.15 · trans. James Legge · source
“Riches and honours are what men desire. If it cannot be obtained in the proper way, they should not be held.”Confucius, Analects, IV.5 · trans. James Legge · source
“The man, who in the view of gain, thinks of righteousness; who in the view of danger is prepared to give up his life; and who does not forget an old agreement however far back it extends”Confucius, Analects, XIV.13 · trans. James Legge · source
“The superior man is anxious lest he should not get truth; he is not anxious lest poverty should come upon him.”Confucius, Analects, XV.31 · trans. James Legge · source

Socrates

“As little foundation is there for the report that I am a teacher, and take money; this accusation has no more truth in it than the other.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Apology · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“I tell you that virtue is not given by money, but that from virtue comes money and every other good of man, public as well as private.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Apology · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“And I myself, Meno, living as I do in this region of poverty, am as poor as the rest of the world; and I confess with shame that I know literally nothing about virtue”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Meno · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Then justice or temperance or holiness, or some other part of virtue, as would appear, must accompany the acquisition, and without them the mere acquisition of good will not be virtue.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Meno · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“And each art gives us a particular good and not merely a general one—medicine, for example, gives us health; navigation, safety at sea, and so on?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic I · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Then why in the case of lesser offices do men never take them willingly without payment, unless under the idea that they govern for the advantage not of themselves but of others?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic I · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source

Marcus Aurelius

“Receive [wealth or prosperity] without arrogance; and be ready to let it go.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VIII.33 · trans. George Long · source
“When thou art offended at any man's fault, forthwith turn to thyself and reflect in what like manner thou dost err thyself; for example, in thinking that money is a good thing, or pleasure, or a bit of reputation, and the like.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, X.30 · trans. George Long · source

Maria Montessori

“An armed brigand stops the carriage with the well-known phrase, "Your money or your life."”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
