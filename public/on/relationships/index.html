What the classics wrote about relationships

Friends, advisers and the people a decision touches: whom to trust, and how to treat them when you disagree.

Every line below appears word for word in the public-domain translation named under it; a script checked each one against the text of that edition. The link opens the letter, book or edition it came from.

Marcus Aurelius

“That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.54 · trans. George Long · source
“Men exist for the sake of one another. Teach them then, or bear with them.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VIII.59 · trans. George Long · source
“The soul of man does violence to itself, first of all, when it becomes an abscess, and, as it were, a tumor on the universe, so far as it can.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, II.16 · trans. George Long · source
“A man then must stand erect, not be kept erect by others.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, III.5 · trans. George Long · source
“All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, II.1 · trans. George Long · source
“Just so then in this life also remember that every duty is made up of certain parts.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.26 · trans. George Long · source
“The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like [the wrong-doer].”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.6 · trans. George Long · source
“Let the same considerations be present to thy mind in the case of the knave, and the faithless man, and of every man who does wrong in any way.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, IX.42 · trans. George Long · source
“If thou art able, correct by teaching those who do wrong; but if thou canst not, remember that indulgence is given to thee for this purpose.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, IX.11 · trans. George Long · source
“Let it not be in any man's power to say truly of thee that thou are not simple or that thou art not good; but let him be a liar whoever shall think anything of this kind about thee;”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, X.32 · trans. George Long · source
“I have often wondered how it is that every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, XII.4 · trans. George Long · source
“For since the universal nature has made rational animals for the sake of one another, to help one another according to their deserts, but in no way to injure one another, he who transgresses her will is clearly guilty of impiety towards the highest divinity.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, IX.1 · trans. George Long · source
“Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink? art thou angry with him whose mouth smells foul? What good will this anger do thee?”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, V.28 · trans. George Long · source
“When a man has done thee any wrong, immediately consider with what opinion about good or evil he has done wrong. For when thou hast seen this, thou wilt pity him, and wilt neither wonder nor be angry.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VII.26 · trans. George Long · source
“So too a man when he is separated from another man has fallen off from the whole social community.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, XI.8 · trans. George Long · source

The Buddha

“For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 5 · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“If a traveller does not meet with one who is his better, or his equal, let him firmly keep to his solitary journey; there is no companionship with a fool.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 61 · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Do not have evil-doers for friends, do not have low people for friends: have virtuous people for friends, have for friends the best of men.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 78 · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“Let a man overcome anger by love, let him overcome evil by good; let him overcome the greedy by liberality, the liar by truth!”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, Dhammapada v. 223 · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“He looks after those in his household, and those dependent on him for their livelihood.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, AN 5.58:12.3–12.4 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“They support you equally in doing bad and doing good. They praise you to your face, and put you down behind your back.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, DN 31:18.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“They tell you secrets. They keep your secrets. They don't abandon you in times of trouble. They'd even give their life for your welfare.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, DN 31:23.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source
“They keep you from doing bad. They support you in doing good. They teach you what you do not know. They explain the path to heaven.”The Buddha, Dhammapada and the suttas, DN 31:24.2 (Sujato) · trans. F. Max Müller; Bhikkhu Sujato · source

Maria Montessori

“This idea of the collective ownership of the school is new and very beautiful and profoundly educational.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. III "Inaugural Address" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“We habitually _serve_ children; and this is not only an act of servility toward them, but it is dangerous, since it tends to suffocate their useful, spontaneous activity.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“Who does not know that to _teach_ a child to feed himself, to wash and dress himself, is a much more tedious and difficult work, calling for infinitely greater patience, than feeding, washing and dressing the child one's self?”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“He is a serene and pleasant man where he is powerful through being efficient, but is domineering where he is served.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. V "Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“not to _insist_ by repeating the lesson; and second, _not to make the child feel that he has made a mistake_, or that he is not understood”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. VI "How the Lessons Should Be Given" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“the normal child, instead, takes spontaneously a lively interest in this game. He pushes away all who would interfere, or offer to help him, and wishes to be alone before his problem.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XII "Education of the Senses" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“If one accomplishes something especially fine, his achievement is a source of admiration and joy to others: no heart suffers from another's wealth, but the triumph of one is a delight to all.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XXI "General Review of Discipline" · trans. Anne E. George · source
“I have seen here, men of affairs, great politicians preoccupied with problems of trade and of state, cast off like an uncomfortable garment the burden of the world, and fall into a simple forgetfulness of self.”Maria Montessori, The Montessori Method, ch. XXII "Conclusions and Impressions" · trans. Anne E. George · source

Seneca

“Everywhere means nowhere. When a person spends all his time in foreign travel, he ends by having many acquaintances, but no friends.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 2 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“When friendship is settled, you must trust; before friendship is formed, you must pass judgment.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 3 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“It is equally faulty to trust everyone and to trust no one.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 3 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“No good thing is pleasant to possess, without friends to share it.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 6 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“He who begins to be your friend because it pays will also cease because it pays.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 9 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“Hence prosperous men are blockaded by troops of friends; but those who have failed stand amid vast loneliness, their friends fleeing from the very crisis which is to test their worth.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 9 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“How closely flattery resembles friendship! It not only apes friendship, but outdoes it, passing it in the race; with wide-open and indulgent ears it is welcomed and sinks to the depths of the heart, and it is pleasing precisely wherein it does harm.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 45 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source
“And no one can live happily who has regard to himself alone and transforms everything into a question of his own utility; you must live for your neighbour, if you would live for yourself.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, Letter 48 · trans. Richard M. Gummere · source

Confucius

“A youth, when at home, should be filial, and, abroad, respectful to his elders. He should be earnest and truthful.”Confucius, Analects, I.6 · trans. James Legge · source
“I will not be afflicted at men's not knowing me; I will be afflicted that I do not know men.”Confucius, Analects, I.16 · trans. James Legge · source
“I do not know how a man without truthfulness is to get on.”Confucius, Analects, II.22 · trans. James Legge · source
“When we see men of worth, we should think of equalling them; when we see men of a contrary character, we should turn inwards and examine ourselves.”Confucius, Analects, IV.17 · trans. James Legge · source
“When I walk along with two others, they may serve me as my teachers. I will select their good qualities and follow them, their bad qualities and avoid them.”Confucius, Analects, VII.21 · trans. James Legge · source
“Recompense injury with justice, and recompense kindness with kindness.”Confucius, Analects, XIV.36 · trans. James Legge · source
“When a man may be spoken with, not to speak to him is to err in reference to the man. When a man may not be spoken with, to speak to him is to err in reference to our words.”Confucius, Analects, XV.7 · trans. James Legge · source

Benjamin Franklin

“retaining only the habit of expressing myself in terms of modest diffidence”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. II (Beginning Life as a Printer) · source
“I wish well-meaning, sensible men would not lessen their power of doing good by a positive, assuming manner, that seldom fails to disgust, tends to create opposition”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. II (Beginning Life as a Printer) · source
“I made it a rule to forbear all direct contradiction to the sentiments of others, and all positive assertion of my own”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. IX (Plan for Attaining Moral Perfection) · source
“The modest way in which I propos'd my opinions procur'd them a readier reception and less contradiction”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. IX (Plan for Attaining Moral Perfection) · source
“Our club, the Junto, was found so useful, and afforded such satisfaction to the members, that several were desirous of introducing their friends”Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography and The Way to Wealth, Autobiography, ch. X (Poor Richard's Almanac and Other Activities) · source

Socrates

“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Crito · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Well, my art of midwifery is in most respects like theirs; but differs, in that I attend men and not women; and look after their souls when they are in labour, and not after their bodies”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Theaetetus · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Reflect: our principle is that the good man will not consider death terrible to any other good man who is his comrade.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic III · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Further, we affirmed that justice was doing one’s own business, and not being a busybody; we said so again and again, and many others have said the same to us.”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic IV · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
“Can there be any greater evil than discord and distraction and plurality where unity ought to reign? or any greater good than the bond of unity?”Socrates, Plato’s dialogues, Republic V · trans. Benjamin Jowett · source
